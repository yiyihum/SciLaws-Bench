Can LLMs Discover Scientific Laws in Real and Parallel Worlds?

SciLaws-Bench grounds law discovery in published research and real measurements — then asks the same question again in a parallel world where the law is new.

Abstract

Scientific equation discovery has long been central to scientific progress, proceeding through iterative cycles of hypothesis generation, observational testing, and refinement under scientific constraints. As LLM capabilities advance and their role in AI for Science expands, it remains an open problem whether they can genuinely discover scientific laws and how this ability should be evaluated. Existing evaluations often either simplify discovery through synthetic settings or reuse published targets that may already be familiar to LLMs.

We introduce SciLaws-Bench, a benchmark for scientific law discovery built from published research and real scientific data. It comprises 118 problems drawn from 381 scientific papers, covering 291 candidate laws and roughly 8M real data points across six scientific disciplines. Each problem is instantiated in two complementary settings: SciLaws-Real asks models to propose laws from fixed real observations and evaluates held-out predictive fit and scientific validity derived from the source literature; SciLaws-Parallel asks models to actively query residual-calibrated worlds and recover synthesized hidden laws derived from published forms.

We find that predictive fit can diverge from scientific validity, that memorization shapes whether models reproduce or move beyond published formulas, and that a best-of-N study reveals a selection bottleneck.

The benchmark

Built from papers, not textbooks

Problems are mined from scientifically active, data-driven literature where published laws still leave room for improvement — not from canonical textbook equations that a model can simply recall.

Two settings, one problem

Real records and parallel worlds

Both settings share the same scientific context, admissible input ranges, validity constraints, and group structure. They differ in where the evidence comes from and what counts as success.

SciLaws-Real

Discovery from fixed observations

The evidence has already been collected. A model reads the scientific context and the training records, then proposes a closed-form law. Published formulas are reference baselines, not recovery targets — models are free to search past them.

  • Numeric fit SN — reference-relative on held-out data. The strongest published formula scores 0.5; a perfect predictor scores 1.0.
  • Scientific validity SV — a code-enabled judge runs each item of a frozen, source-grounded rubric, gated by a global anti-hacking check.
  • Held-out splits — interpolation, input-range extrapolation, temporal transfer, cross-condition generalization, or entirely held-out groups.
SV(t) = at · Mt / Nt   # rubric coverage × anti-hacking gate
SciLaws-Parallel

Discovery by experiment

The same problem becomes a queryable world with a fixed hidden law — a newly synthesized structural variant of a published formula, absent from the literature. Starting with no observations, a model chooses what to measure within a query budget.

  • Structure score SS — five levels: 0 (unrelated), 0.25 (right variables or trends), 0.5 (published base form), 0.75 (base plus most added terms), 1.0 (complete hidden structure).
  • Residual-calibrated simulator — parameters fitted on real records, residuals debiased and resampled by k-NN bootstrap, noise scaled to keep the law identifiable.
  • Active budget — the model picks inputs and, for multi-group tasks, which groups to query.
y = f(X) + α · resample(X)
Results

Nine frontier models, one agent framework

Every model runs inside the same ReAct-style agent: a Python sandbox, up to 30 interaction turns, and — in the Parallel setting — a fixed experimentation budget. Only the base model varies. Scores are percentages; Single = one global law, Multi = a shared functional form with per-group parameters.

The judges behind SV, SS, and the memorization verdict agree with majority labels from five domain experts at Cohen's κ = 0.82, 0.84, and 0.77 — all inside the corresponding human–human ranges.

What we found

Three results

01

Fitting the data well does not make a law scientifically valid

Across 3,616 within-task model pairs with unequal fit, SN and SV agree only 54.9% of the time; agreement reaches 69.5% only once the fit margin exceeds ΔSN ≥ 0.4. On a nuclear-physics task the best-fitting submission introduces a resonance peak that does not exist. On the proton form-factor task all nine models submit physically valid forms, yet seven fail to beat the published reference.

02

Memory supplies the published form; it cannot supply the new terms

A cold-recall audit splits the 118 tasks into Canon (recalled by nearly every model), Mid, and Moat. Only 11.9% are recalled by all nine models, while 47.5% are recalled by none — a discovery moat. Even GPT-5.5 cold-recalls just 34.7%. On Real, models beat published baselines more often on formulas they cannot recall (GPT-5.5: 21% → 54% from Canon to Moat). On Parallel, recall lifts published-form recovery from 0.59 to 0.85, but full-law recovery stays near 0.08 at every tier — and instead rises with capability, from 0% to 21%.

03

Models generate better laws than they can select

Drawing 20 candidates per task and letting the model pick its own winner, oracle selection gains +4.5 pp in fit and +5.3 pp in validity from N=5 to N=20 — while self-selection improves by less than 2 pp on either axis. Within the five-candidate groups shown to the selector, 60% of winners are Pareto-dominated by another candidate the model itself generated. Selection, not generation, is the bottleneck.
